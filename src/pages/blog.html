Mapping 14 February 2026 · 6 min read

Why we map with communities before we build

Our first six weeks in a village produce no concrete, no pipes and no photographs worth posting. They produce a sheet of flipchart paper with a hand-drawn map on it: the school, the two working boreholes, the place where the road floods in April, the footpath the girls take to the market, and the plot the elders argue about every year.

That map is the project brief. When we ask a community to rank their own priorities on it, the answer is often not what a district officer would predict — and it is almost never what our own team assumed before we arrived. In one village in Homa Bay, water ranked third behind a footbridge and a classroom. We had budgeted for water.

The second thing the map gives us is a maintenance list. Beside every asset we agree to build, a name goes down: who fixes it, who collects the small monthly contribution, who we call if it breaks in March. Two of our water committees had already formed themselves before we had drilled anything.

Education 02 February 2026 · 5 min read

Digital skills, not just desks

There is a room in almost every county with eleven donated computers in it, six of which switch on. The donations arrive with good intentions and no budget line for the person who keeps them running.

We costed it properly for one school: a part-time technician at two days a week, a spare power supply unit, a voltage stabiliser, and KES 4,000 a month for internet during term time. That comes to less than the cost of the second-hand machines — and it is the reason the lab was still teaching a full timetable nine months later.

The harder finding: the first cohort who finished the six-week course were mostly boys. The girls were in the room, but they were sharing keyboards and letting the boys type. The next intake ran as two single-sex groups. Attendance for the girls' group was 94%.

Livelihoods 21 January 2026 · 7 min read

Compost, cover crops and a good rainy season

Forty smallholder plots in Migori, two seasons, one rain gauge per cluster. We asked farmers to record three things only: rainfall, what they planted, and what they sold. Nobody had to learn an app — the gauge is read at 7am and written on a card nailed to a post.

The plots that used compost and a cover crop yielded 22% more on average in a season that was actually drier than the year before. But the headline number hides the real story: eleven of the forty farms accounted for almost all of the gain. Those eleven had a younger household member who could carry water and turn the heap.

So the honest conclusion is not "compost works". It is that compost works when there is a pair of hands to manage it, and that our next training round has to be designed around labour, not around technique.
